Sound Map of the Valley

This “sound map” traces several of the key places that appear in my grandmother’s interview. Each entry links a location to a brief audio memory and the kind of work, feeling, or routine that takes place there. Instead of drawing the border as a line, it shows how the Valley is organized by paths, errands, and stories that move back and forth.

Boca Chica Beach

Childhood coastline  ·  Camping, fishing, swimming

“As a little girl, our family would go down to Boca Chica… everybody would go down there and fish and camp and swim.”

As a child, she remembers Boca Chica as a sparse beach where families camped, fished, and chased sand crabs. The same site now hosts SpaceX infrastructure. The shift from wash tubs of crabs to launch pads condenses a larger story: the Valley repositioned from local recreation ground to a stage for global technology and investment.

Brownsville & Matamoros Bridge

Everyday crossing  ·  Lunch, errands, school

“As teenagers, we went across the bridge to have lunch or dinner or, you know, shop… there was no fear.”

The bridge appears as a routine route: teenagers walk or drive across for lunch, shopping, or visits. The movement is unremarkable and low-stress, which reveals a period when inspection and sovereignty mattered, but did not define the meaning of the crossing. The bridge in her memory is closer to a neighborhood street than a fortified threshold.

Downtown Brownsville & Market Square

Work & enforcement  ·  Meat market, part-time police

“He also had a meat market in the downtown Brownsville Market Square… so they knew he was a very good shot.”

Her grandfather’s meat market and part-time police work took place in the downtown Market Square. It becomes the setting for the duel story, where a friend calls on his reputation with a gun to settle a dispute. The same streets hold formal law, informal enforcement, and everyday commerce, showing how authority circulated through proximity and personal standing as much as through institutions.

Old City Cemetery, Brownsville

Ritual space  ·  Fiesta de Vida

“They had a lovely celebration in the Old City Cemetery, but they called it Fiesta de Vida, Festival of Life.”

The Old City Cemetery anchors multigenerational memory: relatives lost in the 1918 flu, the earlier Elizabeth she is named after, and the present-day Día de los Muertos celebration renamed Fiesta de Vida. It is a place where private mourning and public festivity come together, showing how the Valley makes remembrance a communal act rather than a strictly private one.

Fruit Tree Corridor Between Houses

Domestic border  ·  Childhood autonomy

“We had a row of fruit trees that went from our house to her house… I’d run back to my grandmother’s in the morning.”

A row of fruit trees connects her house to her grandmother’s. As a girl, she runs this corridor in the mornings to have her hair braided. The route is short, but it teaches independent movement and everyday trust. In a project about borders, this domestic path matters because it shows how children learn to navigate space confidently long before they confront national lines.

Fort Brown, Brownsville

Military node  ·  124th Cavalry

“He joined the Texas National Guard and got sent to Fort Brown… the last horse-mounted cavalry in the United States Army.”

Fort Brown is where her father serves in the last horse-mounted cavalry and where he hears about Pearl Harbor at a bait stand on the way to the beach. The site links the Valley’s local economy and leisure to global war history, showing how national conflict passes through border garrisons without erasing their everyday routines.

Shrimp Docks & Gulf Coast

Work, risk, and story  ·  Shrimp boats, rum-running

“We had a couple of shrimp boats… the shrimping industry was across the river and in the United States… we’d go down as far as Tampico and shrimp.”

The shrimp industry binds Brownsville to Tampico, to Florida, and to prohibition-era rum routes. Her stories of Louisiana shrimpers, rum-running to Chicago, and train cars to the Kentucky Derby sit alongside the more ordinary labor of hauling nets. The docks are a maritime border, where risk, opportunity, and storytelling travel together.
