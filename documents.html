These excerpts come from a longer oral history interview with my grandmother, Elizabeth Ressmann, about growing up in Brownsville and moving back and forth between Brownsville and Matamoros. I have chosen each passage because it shows how language, work, and ritual quietly organized everyday life on the border.

The brief notes that follow are not summaries of what she says. They are short analytic readings that connect her memories to the course themes of hybrid language, cross-border movement, informal governance, and changing senses of belonging. In Alessandro Portelli’s terms, oral history is an “argument about meaning,” and these excerpts are the parts of the interview that most clearly stage that argument.

Everyday Bilingualism

“Everyone almost was bilingual because that’s just how you grew up… there was not a distinction really between Anglo and Mexican American or Mexican national.”

Here bilingualism appears as a taken-for-granted condition rather than a special skill. Her phrasing “that’s just how you grew up” treats switching between languages as ordinary maintenance work that keeps schools, shops, and households running. This complicates national narratives that imagine English and Spanish as competing blocks; in her memory, they are parts of the same practical toolkit.

Tex-Mex as Method

“I really learned Spanish before I learned English… they would say, ‘watcha le’… it was Tex-Mex… ‘más o menos’… it was a very natural type of language… completely intermixed most of the time.”

The phrases she chooses—“watcha le,” “más o menos”—carry instructions more than identities: be careful, approximate, adjust. What Gloria Anzaldúa, in Borderlands/La Frontera, theorizes as a border tongue that signals mixed identity appears here as a working language for problem-solving. Tex-Mex is not ornament; it is a method for staying flexible in a setting where people, goods, and obligations constantly move.

Cross-Border Routine

“As teenagers, we went across the bridge to have lunch or dinner or shop… there was no fear… the proprietors treated us great. And we treated them great.”

The bridge crossing is framed as a casual trip for lunch, not an exceptional event. That tone of routine matters. It shows a period when the physical infrastructure of the border was already present, yet the social meaning of crossing was grounded in recognition and hospitality. Teenagers and shopkeepers share obligations: respectful behavior, fair dealing, and the expectation that the relationship will continue.

Negotiation at the Lowest Level

“You just talked to somebody and got it figured out. That was it.”

This line condenses an entire model of governance. Problems are meant to be solved face-to-face by people who know one another, rather than by distant officials or layers of paperwork. It resonates with Francisco Cantú’s descriptions, in The Line Becomes a River, of how informal understandings still operate beneath formal enforcement. In her memory, the “figuring out” happens quickly and at ground level, which makes later shifts toward securitized and militarized solutions feel like a rupture.

Reciprocity Without Paperwork

“She got stuck… along comes Don Chucho with his little cart and his donkeys… he used some of his wood to put under the tires… whenever my grandmother saw him, she’d take him a pot of soup or an extra fish.”

The scene with Don Chucho stages an unwritten contract. A stranger uses his tools and time to pull a car out of the flats; the family answers with food over many years. No money changes hands, but a durable tie is created. This kind of reciprocal obligation rarely appears in official histories, yet it is central to how people experience a place as safe, knowable, and worth staying in.

Gender and Independence

“There was more of the traditional… Mama stayed home… but my mother worked some. One thing she always told me was, ‘have some of your own money.’… I always felt very independent.”

Her account aligns household roles with regional tradition, yet her mother quietly revises those expectations through advice about money. The insistence on “your own money” trains a habit of self-reliance that sits inside, not outside, a conventional family structure. In the context of the border, that independence extends beyond the home: it supports crossing, working, and navigating institutions without asking permission.

Ritual Memory

“We would take flowers to the cemetery… when I went down for this Día de los Muertos… they called it Fiesta de Vida… people still remember their relatives and talk about them.”

The Old City Cemetery links the 1918 flu, her ancestor Elizabeth, and present-day Fiesta de Vida in a single space. Children carrying roses in tin buckets and adults decorating graves for Día de los Muertos are part of the same long ritual. The cemetery becomes a kind of open-air archive where private mourning and public celebration meet, and where Brownsville’s past is retold in family-scale scenes rather than in official monuments.

Change and Estrangement

“When I go down there now and see SpaceX trying to send people to Mars, it’s just incredible to me… there’s just not that sense of belonging and closeness like there used to be.”

SpaceX’s launch complex at Boca Chica stands on top of her childhood beach. In one sentence she moves from amazement at the technology to a quieter grief about lost closeness. The juxtaposition captures a core tension of contemporary borderlands: global projects and speculative futures arrive on landscapes layered with local memory, and the new use of the land does not necessarily preserve the older forms of community that made it feel like home.
