This project begins from a question about how border life becomes livable. The podcast at the center of this project draws on a fifty-four-minute oral history interview with my grandmother, Elizabeth Ressmann, who grew up in Brownsville in the 1950s and crossed regularly into Matamoros, Mexico. Rather than treating her memories as nostalgic anecdotes, I approached them as evidence of how bilingual talk, small-scale negotiations, and everyday movement created a workable civic world long before “the border” became a national symbol of crisis.

The podcast format allows listeners to hear tone, hesitations, emphasis, and humor. These are features that written transcription cannot reproduce. As oral historian Alessandro Portelli notes, the value of spoken testimony lies in the texture of recollection. This takes shape in the cadences and the interpretive decisions people make as they narrate their histories. In my grandmother’s account, bilingual phrases, small jokes, generously detailed scenes, and sudden shifts in mood all signal a space shaped by movement rather than division.

The accompanying materials on the website serve distinct purposes. The transcript excerpts spotlight specific moments of linguistic hybridity and reciprocity, using short analytic notes to connect those moments to the course themes. The sound map takes a different angle, situating her memories in the geography that produced them. It shows how places become meaningful through the routines that move across the river. That movement appears in anecdotes of hair-braiding routes under fruit trees, shrimping trips to Tampico, and walks across the bridge for lunch.

The poem, written in the spirit of Alberto Ríos, offers a compressed version of the world she describes. Ríos’ poems often take small domestic images such as fruit, light, and fences and let them open outward toward cultural and historical meaning. In an attempt to follow his approach, I used the image of the fruit tree corridor between her house and her grandmother’s as a metaphor for how border identities are learned through small, repeated motions.

The visual essay extends this work by bringing in three photographs by Tommy Kha, whose portraits often stage the tension between attachment and estrangement. His images of costuming, fragmented bodies, and altered domestic interiors helped me think about belonging and dislocation in relation to my grandmother’s stories. Kha’s photographs expose the negotiations that persist beneath the surface of home and region. In placing these images alongside the podcast, I am able to see the border as a place where identity is continuously assembled and adjusted.

The family and regional photo archive broadens this visual inquiry by rooting the project in the textures of lived history. This takes shape in backyard citrus harvests, Charro Days costumes, and Boca Chica beaches. Unlike the visual essay, which works through staged portraiture and artistic interpretation, the archive offers documentary fragments that ground my grandmother’s story in the material world that produced it. These images serve as informal historical records of everyday performance. Placed alongside the oral history, they help show how memory is sustained through objects and images as much as through narrative.

Across the podcast, the documents page, the poem, the sound map, and the visual essay, I tried to honor the specificity of her memories while framing them within the scholarship surrounding the border. Primarily, I have grounded the framing of my work in Anzaldúa’s Borderlands/La Frontera, Ríos’ essays and poems on everyday revelation, David Spener’s work on undocumented crossings (Clandestine Crossings), and scholarship on Rio Grande Valley history. Each of these writers helped me recognize what my grandmother’s stories were already doing. They preserve a regional way of life that functioned through repetition and shared competence.

The final project functions as a reconstruction of a local way of living that has changed unevenly across time. I aimed to show how memory works as both evidence and inheritance through my pairing of oral history with analysis, poetry, spatial representation, and visual inquiry. What my grandmother describes is a set of practices that still inform how South Texans understand themselves, even as the border around them becomes louder, harder, and more surveilled. My hope is that this project makes clear how much of that quieter world remains and how much these older forms of competence still shape the feeling of belonging.

With thanks to Dr. Driscoll for her guidance throughout this project.
