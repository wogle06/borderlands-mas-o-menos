Project Overview

This project centers on an oral history interview with my grandmother, Elizabeth Ressmann, who grew up in Brownsville in the 1950s. The podcast uses her memories to describe how people in Brownsville and Matamoros relied on bilingual conversation, cross-border routines, and small acts of mutual help to make everyday life workable in a region shaped by movement rather than division.

The supporting materials—a brief poem written in the style of Alberto Ríos, selected transcript excerpts, a photo gallery, and a simple sound map—are not illustrations of the podcast but companion pieces. Each one highlights a different aspect of the world she describes: its language, its routes, and its sense of belonging. Together, they offer a grounded look at how border life was lived and remembered across generations.

How to Navigate This Project

Begin with the podcast. It introduces the oral history, the broader themes, and the interpretive frame that guides the rest of the project. After listening, explore the transcript excerpts, the sound map, and the visual essay, which expand on particular moments and locations from the interview. The poem offers a compressed, metaphorical version of the world described in the oral history. The artist statement comes last, bringing the materials together and exlaining how the project's components work across audio, text, image, and analysis.

Fruit Trees Between Two Countries

A poem, written in the spirit of Alberto Ríos, to accompany the interview:

The border was not a line for her
but a row of fruit trees,
the ones that ran from her house
to her grandmother’s,
each tree carrying a season,
each season carrying permission:
run, child, run,
the world is close
and waiting.

Brownsville spoke
in two languages at once.
Words leaned on each other
like neighbors at a fence,
English resting on Spanish,
Spanish borrowing the shade
of an English syllable,
everybody hearing both sides
of the sentence.

Her stories say the river
did not divide anyone.
It just helped them choose
where to eat lunch.

In Matamoros the streets opened
like familiar rooms,
and shopkeepers knew her face
the way the tide knows shore
coming, going, returning
without ceremony.

Some days the world tilted wild
a burro riding an elevator
during Charro Days,
shrimp boats whispering north,
her grandmother’s car stuck in the flats
until Don Chucho arrived
with his donkeys and his quiet patience.
A pot of soup would answer the favor.

But most days the Valley held together
with small stitches:
a borrowed phrase,
a hand steadying a wheel,
somebody saying “I’ll help you”
as if it were part of the weather.

She tells me this as memory,
but I hear it as instruction
a map made of gestures,
a geography of kindness
and quick understanding.

And I wonder how much of that place
is still carried in us,
how much fruit still grows
between two countries,
how many simple paths remain
for us to follow home.

Borders & Borderlands final project — William Ogle
